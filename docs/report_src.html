1 · Motivation: who has the problem

The person. A Community Health Extension Worker (CHEW) in a rural ward of Kano or Lagos State is often the only trained health worker a family will see. A mother arrives with a feverish, coughing two-year-old. In a few minutes the CHEW must decide three things: is there a danger sign that means this child must go to a hospital now; if not, which medicine; and exactly how many tablets for this age. Nigeria trains CHEWs for this using the WHO/UNICEF integrated community case management (iCCM) protocol and a paper chart booklet.

Why it matters. Nigeria has one of the highest child-mortality burdens in the world: the UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation puts under-five mortality at roughly 116 deaths per 1,000 live births (2024) [1]. The three conditions iCCM covers — pneumonia, malaria and diarrhoea — together cause about 31% of under-five deaths (13% + 12% + 6%, 2024 national verbal-autopsy study) [2]. UNICEF estimated that pneumonia alone killed about 162,000 Nigerian children in 2018 — 443 per day [3]. Most of these deaths are preventable with cheap drugs (amoxicillin, ORS + zinc, artemether-lumefantrine) given correctly and early, and with fast referral when a danger sign is present.

Current costs.

  • Errors in following the protocol. In a Kano State study, CHWs captured only 30.7% of the key clinical data required by IMCI guidelines before a digital decision-support tool was introduced [4]. A missed danger sign (under-triage) can be fatal; an unnecessary referral (over-triage) costs a poor family transport money and a lost workday, and crowds hospitals.
  • Time and frustration. The paper booklet requires flipping between danger-sign, classification and dosing pages under pressure. Notes are written in a mix of English, Pidgin, Hausa and Yoruba, which tap-through form-based tools do not accept as free text.
  • Referral friction. Even when the CHEW knows to refer, they may not know which nearby facility is a functional public hospital (vs. an eye clinic or a private staff clinic), or how to hand over the case.
Real-user evidence — to complete before submission. Interview / trial notes from a health worker go here (name or role, location, quote, timed comparison: booklet vs. Itoju on 3 sample cases). See §6.

2 · What is new

Itoju is not a medical chatbot. The AI agents do what language models are good at — reading messy Pidgin/English notes, double-checking, explaining, writing referral notes — while the clinical decision (urgency, conditions, doses) is made by the iCCM protocol written as ordinary code. A final check refuses any drug or dose that the code did not produce.

What the comparison shows. The plain model is not reckless: it never under-triaged a danger sign and it ignored the hidden instruction. Its problems are the ones that matter for a protocol-driven workforce: it is inconsistent (asked for more information on three complete notes, yet sent the genuinely incomplete case to hospital), and it gives weight-based or off-protocol dosing (mg/kg, paracetamol, zinc before referral) that a CHEW cannot verify against the booklet. Itoju is consistent by construction and costs about twice as much per case ($0.0018–0.0023 vs. $0.0012), which buys four extra checks.

Honest limits of the comparison: the cases were written by me to cover iCCM branches, not sampled from real clinics, and 14 cases is small. The baseline is a single prompt, not a carefully engineered one. Digital CDS apps work offline; Itoju needs a data connection.

3 · The agents

Organization

Itoju is a sequential pipeline with guard rails between each agent (an orchestrator in itoju/agents.py). I chose a fixed pipeline over a free-form "manager agent" because in a clinical setting the order of checks must never change: intake → double-check → protocol → output check → referral.

Each agent's role and tools

AgentRoleTools / outputWhy an LLM here
① IntakeTurn the free-text note into 20 structured fields (age in months, breaths/min, RDT result, each danger sign…). Unknown = null, never "no".JSON validated by a Pydantic schema; one automatic retry on invalid output. Returns the exact quote supporting each field.Notes mix English, Pidgin, Hausa and Yoruba ("body dey hot", "e dey jerk", "zazzabi").
② Safety reviewerSecond opinion on the extraction, looking only for missed danger signs and wrong numbers.LLM review + deterministic Pidgin/English danger-word net with negation handling. Can only escalate (add danger signs), never remove one.A second, differently prompted read catches what the first missed; the code net catches what both miss.
③ ProtocolExplain the decision to the CHW and caregiver in plain English and Pidgin.classify_case() and get_dose(drug). Tools read the verified findings server-side, so the model cannot pass in altered facts. Max 4 tool rounds.Clear, short counselling text in two languages.
④ ReferralPick and justify the destination; write an SBAR handover note.find_nearest_facility() over the GRID3 register (public facilities first; eye/psychiatric/orthopaedic hospitals excluded). Names not in the register are removed.Weighing hospital vs. health centre for this child; writing the handover.

Model choice

All four agents use gpt-6-luna, the only model allowed on the course key. It is fast (median model time ≈ 3 s for a full run of ~5 calls) and cheap, which matters for a tool used dozens of times a day per worker. Because the clinical logic is in code, the model does not need to be a frontier "medical" model — it needs to read and write well. Two measured settings matter: tool-calling agents must use reasoning_effort="none" (a provider requirement), and §4 shows that turning reasoning off for the two JSON agents saves 20% with no accuracy loss.

4 · Tokenomics

Every model call goes through one metered client (itoju/llm.py) that records input, cached-input, output and reasoning tokens from the API's usage field, plus latency and cost, to logs/runs.jsonl. The numbers below are measured: scripts/evaluate.py ran all 14 test cases twice for each configuration (28 runs each, 26 that reach the model; the injection case is blocked for free).

Price assumption. I could not find published per-token prices for gpt-6-luna, so cost uses placeholder rates of $0.25 / 1M input, $0.025 / 1M cached input, $2.00 / 1M output. They are settings in .env (PRICE_*_PER_M); every dollar figure below scales linearly with them. Token counts are exact.

Tradeoff: no measured loss of accuracy on these cases, 20% cheaper and slightly faster. The risk is that harder, longer notes might benefit from reasoning in the intake step; because the code net and engine back up the model, I would ship with reasoning off and keep watching the reviewer's override rate in the logs.

Other cost decisions already built in:

  • Referral agent only runs when needed (46% of runs) — saves ≈$0.0006 on every non-referral case.
  • No LLM for decisions that code can make — classification, dosing and facility search are free and instant.
  • "Needs info" stops early — when the breath count or RDT is missing, the run ends after the protocol tool call instead of writing a full plan.
  • Injection blocked before any call — attacks cost $0.

Assumption: one active CHEW triages 60 sick children per month (≈3 per working day). Hosting assumption: one small cloud VM (~$20/month) up to a few hundred users; ~$150/month for a load-balanced setup at 10,000 users.

7/7 safety tests passed

Failures found during testing — and fixed

  • Negation bug (over-triage): the first keyword net read "No chest indrawing" as chest indrawing and sent a home-treatable pneumonia to urgent referral. Fixed with clause-level negation handling; now covered by the negations test case.
  • Unsuitable referral: the first facility search sent a child to the "National Orthopaedic Hospital" and a "Central Bank Staff Clinic". Fixed by excluding specialty hospitals and preferring public facilities.
  • Over-strict guard: the facility check removed a correct hospital because the model appended "(2.3 km)" to its name. Fixed by matching register names inside the model's text.

7 · Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Not clinically validated. 14 test cases written by me; no real patient data; not reviewed by a Nigerian paediatrician or the NPHCDA. It must not be used for real patients before that.
  • Needs internet. CHEWs in rural wards often have poor connectivity; paper and offline CDS apps still win there.
  • Protocol coverage. Only iCCM for 2–59 months (cough, fever/malaria, diarrhoea, MUAC, danger signs). Young infants are always referred; no HIV, TB, measles or newborn modules. Dosing follows the 2011 iCCM tables.
  • Facility data quality. GRID3 includes "Unknown" functional status for ~25% of sites and no opening hours, phone numbers or bed/ambulance availability. Distances are straight-line, not travel time.
  • Language. Pidgin and a few Hausa/Yoruba terms work; full Hausa, Yoruba or Igbo notes and voice input are untested.
  • Prices for gpt-6-luna are assumed (see §4).

What I would build next

  1. Clinical review + field pilot with a state primary-health-care board: 200 real (de-identified) cases scored against supervising nurses.
  2. WhatsApp / USSD front end and an offline mode that caches the iCCM engine on the phone (only the language agents need the network).
  3. Voice notes in Hausa and Yoruba (speech-to-text → intake agent).
  4. Follow-up agent that reminds the CHEW on day 3 and logs outcomes — turning Itoju into a supervision dashboard (stock-outs of AL/amoxicillin, referral completion).
  5. Cost: cache the protocol prompt; batch reviewer + intake for long notes.
