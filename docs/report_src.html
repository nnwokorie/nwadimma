1 · Motivation: who has the problem

The context (for readers outside Nigeria). Nigeria has the highest number of maternal deaths of any country: roughly 29% of all maternal deaths worldwide — about one in four — at a ratio of 993 deaths per 100,000 live births in 20231, compared with about 19 in the United States. Worldwide, the leading causes are severe bleeding (27%) and pre-eclampsia/eclampsia (16%), followed by infection and obstructed labour2. Almost all of these deaths are treatable at a hospital that can operate and give blood — if the woman gets there in time. Only about 4 in 10 Nigerian births happen in a health facility3.

Why women don't get there: the three delays. The classic framework for maternal death4 names three delays. Nwa Dị Mma targets the first two:

1 · Deciding to goNot recognising a danger sign; waiting for a husband's permission; hoping it passes by morning.
2 · Reaching the right placeGoing first to a clinic or maternity home that cannot do a caesarean or transfusion, then being sent on.
3 · Receiving careStaff, blood and drugs at the hospital. Out of scope, but the handover note shortens it.

The person. A community health extension worker (CHEW), a traditional birth attendant, or a relative at a home birth in Enugu, Kano or Lagos. The woman has been in labour "since yesterday", or has "soaked three wrappers" after delivering at home, or has a headache "wey no gree" at eight months. The helper must decide, often at night: is this normal, or must she go now — and where? Conversations happen in English, Pidgin, Igbo, Hausa or Yoruba.

Current costs. Delay costs lives: postpartum haemorrhage can kill within hours. Going to the wrong facility costs time and money — a woman taken first to a small maternity home is referred on, often in a second hired vehicle. Over-referral has a cost too: a poor family pays transport and lost work for an unnecessary hospital trip, which teaches them not to go next time.

2 · What is new

Nwa Dị Mma is not a pregnancy chatbot. The AI agents do what language models are good at — reading messy Pidgin/Igbo/English descriptions, double-checking, explaining to the family in their language, writing a handover note. The decision itself (how urgent, where to go, which pre-referral medicine) is made by WHO danger-sign rules written as ordinary code, and a final check refuses any medicine or dose the code did not produce. Its distinctive move is choosing the right destination: an emergency goes to a hospital that can operate, not to the nearest clinic.

3 · The agents

Organization

A sequential pipeline with guard rails between each agent (orchestrator in itoju/agents.py). In an emergency the order of checks must never change, so I chose a fixed pipeline over a free-form manager agent.

Model choice

All four agents use gpt-6-luna, the only model allowed on the course key. It is fast and cheap (≈{{CALLS}} calls and ≈{{LAT_DEF}} s of model time per case), which matters for a tool used at 2 a.m. on a basic phone. Because the clinical logic is in code, the model does not need medical expertise — it needs to read and write well in three languages. Failures found while building — and fixed

  • Negation (over-triage): an early keyword net read "No chest indrawing" as a danger sign. Fixed with clause-level negation handling ("no bleeding", "she no dey bleed"); covered by the negations case.
  • Wrong destination: the first facility search picked an orthopaedic hospital, a bank staff clinic and a nursing school; GRID3 also labels many small private "specialist hospitals" as state-owned. Fixed by excluding unsuitable facilities and preferring general/teaching/federal hospitals within 25 km.
  • Over-strict guard: the facility check removed a correct hospital because the model appended "(2.3 km)" to its name. Fixed by matching register names inside the model's text.
  • Ambiguous Igbo: "ama jijiji" (trembling) was first mapped to convulsions; it also means shivering with fever, so I removed it to avoid false alarms.

7 · Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Not clinically validated. 17 cases written by me; no real patient data; not reviewed by a Nigerian obstetrician, midwife or the NPHCDA. It must not be used for real patients before that.
  • No real capability data. GRID3 has no flag for caesarean section, blood bank or 24-hour staffing, so "can handle emergencies" is a proxy (general/teaching/federal hospitals). ~25% of sites have unknown functional status; distances are straight-line, not travel time at night.
  • Needs internet, and the decision is only as good as the description — the helper still has to look, ask and ideally measure blood pressure.
  • Medicines: only three WHO pre-referral options, only for trained staff; the app never replaces a midwife's judgement.
  • Language: Igbo messages still need review by more native speakers (dialects differ); Hausa and Yoruba are recognised as terms only; no voice input.
  • Prices for gpt-6-luna are assumed (see §4).

What I would build next

  1. Clinical review and a pilot with a state primary-health-care board and midwives: 200 real (de-identified) cases scored against senior midwives.
  2. Live capability map: hospitals mark "theatre open / blood available tonight", so routing uses real capacity, not a proxy.
  3. Transport hand-off: one tap to call a community emergency-transport driver with the destination and handover note.
  4. WhatsApp and voice notes in Igbo, Hausa and Yoruba; offline rules on the phone.
  5. Birth-plan follow-up: reminders in the last month of pregnancy and a postnatal check at 48 hours.
